Carol Dweck

The view you adopt for yourself profoundly affects the way you lead your life.

Her core finding: what people believe about their abilities shapes whether they actually develop them.

  • I don't know what your view on intelligence is, your own, others' and how it is structured, etc.
  • Professor of Psychology at Stanford University and a leading researcher on motivation and mindsets.
  • Her work focuses on why people succeed and how it is possible to foster their success.
  • In the context of education, she seeks to understand why some students give up in the face of failure, while others thrive.
  • Over the past three decades, her research has shown that the way students think about intelligence and their ability affects their motivation and achievement in school.
  • She has demonstrated that changing students' mindsets in certain ways can have a lasting impact.

Two mindsets

🔒 Fixed

  • "I'm just not a maths person"
  • Talent is innate
  • Effort = you're not smart enough
  • Avoids challenge
  • Feedback feels like judgement

🌱 Growth

  • "I can't do this yet"
  • Abilities are built
  • Effort = the path to mastery
  • Seeks challenge
  • Feedback is information
  • These aren't personality types — we ALL have both, in different areas.
  • Key word from Dweck: "yet." That single word transforms a dead-end into a path.

The power of "yet"

❌ "I can't do this."

Full stop. The story ends. Identity is fixed. The brain disengages.

✅ "I can't do this yet."

A path opens. The story continues. The brain stays curious.

Dweck's studies with children showed that simply reframing with "yet" changed persistence, performance, and even brain activity.

  • "Yet" is not just positive thinking — it shifts the brain from threat state to learning state.
  • Dweck measured this with EEG: kids hearing "yet" showed more activity in error-processing regions.

The story you tell yourself

Every experience gets a narrative. You're the author.

Fixed narrative

"I failed the test → I'm stupid → Why bother trying"

"They got promoted, not me → I'm not good enough"

Growth narrative

"I failed the test → What did I miss? → Next time I'll study differently"

"They got promoted → What can I learn from their approach?"

Same event. Different story. Different outcome.

  • This ties to cognitive behavioural therapy — the meaning we assign to events determines our emotional and behavioural response.
  • The growth mindset is essentially choosing a story that keeps you in the game.

How we actually learn

Mass learning (cramming)

One long session, everything at once. Feels productive, fades fast.

Spaced learning

Shorter sessions, spread over time. Each return strengthens the spiral.

The gaps between sessions are not wasted time: where consolidation happens.

  • It's how anki and duolingo work: spaced repetition software leverages this principle.

  • Revisiting is not a sign of failure. It is the mechanism.

  • Bruner's spiral curriculum concept: you revisit topics at increasing depth.
  • Ebbinghaus's spacing effect (1885): distributed practice produces stronger long-term retention than massed practice.
  • This is why cramming works for an exam but not for lasting knowledge.
  • "I already learned this and still don't get it" is a fixed-mindset trap — you're supposed to revisit it.
  • Each return through the spiral builds on the last. The forgetting between sessions is part of the process: it forces the brain to actively reconstruct, which deepens the memory trace.

Growth happens at the edge of your ability.

Too easy → boredom. Too hard → shutdown.
The stretch zone is where mindset matters most.

  • Vygotsky's Zone of Proximal Development. Csikszentmihalyi's flow.
  • If the challenge is right-sized, struggle feels productive rather than defeating.
  • This is where the growth mindset gives you permission to stay longer.

How praise shapes mindset

Person praise
"You're so smart!"

→ Kids chose easier tasks, lied about scores, collapsed at difficulty

Process praise
"You worked really hard on that strategy!"

→ Kids chose harder tasks, persisted, improved

One sentence of praise shifted children's mindset and behaviour.

  • The study: children did puzzles, then received either person praise or process praise.
  • On the next task, "smart" kids avoided challenge. "Hard-working" kids sought it.
  • This applies to how we talk to ourselves too — internal validation follows the same pattern.

The "genius" myth

We don't apply praise equally. There's a gendered pattern:

Boys

Success → "He's a natural"
Failure → "He didn't try hard enough"

Ability is assumed. Effort explains the gap.

Girls

Success → "She worked so hard"
Failure → "She's just not got it"

Effort is assumed. Ability is in question.

  • The genius myth: the belief that success comes from innate brilliance rather than effort and strategy.
  • Teachers, parents, and children themselves show this pattern.
  • Boys' successes attributed to ability, failures to lack of effort (protects self-image).
  • Girls' successes attributed to effort, failures to lack of ability (erodes self-image).
  • This is not about actual ability — it's about the story we tell about where ability comes from.
  • "Inspiration exists, but it has to find you working."

Consequences

By age 6, girls already associate "brilliance" with boys, not themselves.

They begin to avoid activities described as being for children who are "really, really smart."

Fields that prize "innate genius" have the largest gender gaps.

STEM, philosophy, ...: where culture signals they don't belong.

  • Bian, Leslie & Cimpian (2017): by age 6, girls are less likely to believe members of their gender are "really, really smart" and start avoiding activities labelled as requiring brilliance.
  • Leslie et al. (2015): across 30 academic disciplines, the more a field prizes innate brilliance, the fewer women (and Black people) are in it.
  • Growth mindset directly counters this: if ability is built, not born, then effort is the path, not the consolation prize.

External → Internal

Where does your validation come from?

External validation

"Do they think I'm good enough?"
"Did I get the grade/promotion/like?"

Fragile. You can't control it. It trains you to perform, not to learn.

Internal validation

"Did I improve?"
"Did I give honest effort?"
"What did I learn?"

Durable. You own it. It trains you to grow.

Growth mindset = shifting the scoreboard inside.

  • This is the self-talk connection. Fixed mindset people tend to seek external proof of worth.
  • Growth mindset people build an internal compass.
  • Neither is absolute — we all need some external feedback — but the balance matters.

Rewriting your story: a toolkit

When you hear yourself say "I'm bad at X", try one of these:

These are concrete moves, not just positive thinking. Each one disrupts the fixed-mindset loop differently:

  • "Yet" opens a timeline.
  • "Get specific" breaks a vague identity into a trainable skill.
  • "Blame strategy" separates self-worth from method.

Add "yet"

"I haven't got the hang of this yet"

Get specific

"I tend to rush the opening — I can practise that part"

Blame the strategy, not yourself

"I haven't found the right approach for me yet"

Trust the spiral

"I've been here before and understood more each time"

Find the smallest step

"What's one tiny thing I could try this week?"

Shift the scoreboard

"Am I better at this than six months ago?"

  • "Spiral" normalises revisiting.
  • "Smallest step" defeats overwhelm.
  • "Shift scoreboard" moves from external comparison to internal progress.
